The following is sparked by Col's recent post - [The gap between knowing and deciding](https://beerc.wordpress.com/2026/05/27/the-gap-between-knowing-and-deciding/) in which he bemoans decision making in universities for relying (almost) entirely on authority for decision-making, rather than expertise. Amongst much else he suggests [democratic professionalism](https://www.ei-ie.org/en/item/26128:the-new-democratic-professional-confronting-markets-metrics-and-managerialism) as an option of individual action.

Early in my higher ed career teaching staff were left to proceed solely on their own expertise. That worked for some, but not most. For many, it was perhaps another example of expertise through authority. Toward the end of my career teaching staff were required to have expertise, usually in the form of a Graduate Certificate or some such. Given on-going complaints about the quality of learning and teaching in higher ed that additional expertise doesn't appear to have helped decision making.

IMHO, a significant reason for this has been the significant explosion in complexity of learning and teaching between the early 1990s and today. An explosion that has arisen from what appears to be an almost global trajectory in higher education. A trajectory illustrated in the following image that combines a few sections 

_a false binary?_ The problem is that the level of expertise required to develop effectively learning and teaching is too diverse. Making the challenge an "iron triangle" problem. How to be cost efficient, good quality, and broadly accessible.

There's a question whether you can actually do this. But if you can, it's likely going to require the effective weaving together of multiple different types of expertise.


_my old man role to ponder_

_my favourite times in higher ed was the late 1990s through early 2000s. A time when I could engage in this sort of work. A last gasp of when deciding was left to the academics._

_But it was a glory age. Especially in L&T a lot of academics were not experts or at least couldn't be bothered to apply (let alone develop) what expertise they had._

_It was perhaps worse at the institution I worked because it was a dual-mode university. The program within which I taught offered courses to both on-campus and distance students_
